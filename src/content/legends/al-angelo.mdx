## A Second Father to a Generation of Pioneers

Al Angelo, widely considered the most respected football coach in Philadelphia high school history, passed away on April 19, 2008, at the age of 77. He had spent more than a year in a courageous battle with cancer, and through all of it, his former players stayed close — calling, visiting, writing — the way they had for decades. To the Frankford men who wore his colors, Angelo wasn't just a coach. He was a second father, and for many, a first one.

He holds the city record for most championships won — 10 Public League titles in 21 seasons at Frankford High (1965-84, 1987) — and his numbers for winning percentage (.833) and wins-per-season (8.8) sit near the very top of every all-time list ever assembled for the city's coaches.

{{PhotoInline id="angelo-portrait"}}

A Frankford High graduate himself, Angelo transferred to West Chester University after a shoulder injury ended his playing career at Mississippi State. He coached Frankford's junior varsity alongside his lifelong friend Vince McAneney in college, returned to Frankford as an assistant from 1962-64, and took over as head coach in 1965. He also chaired the school's boys physical education department. He never left.

{{PullQuote id="players-team"}}

## The Early Years: Learning to Win

Angelo's career did not begin with dynasty. Frankford went 4-5-1 in his first season and 4-5-1 again in 1968 — losing records in two of his first four years. Then something clicked. From 1969 through his final whistle in 1987, the Pioneers went 164-22-3. Angelo won his first Public League title in 1969, when blocking wingback Craig Henry caught three touchdown passes from Warren Mays in the title game, and another in 1971, when the Pioneers junked the wing-T for an I formation and handed Gary Hegh the ball 24 times. He piled up championships the way his players piled up two-a-day reps — the 1972 team beat Mastbaum on a fourth-quarter drive; the 1973 team scored three touchdowns in the final 3:52 to beat Southern 14-13; the 1975 team outran Mastbaum behind George Benson's 170-yard afternoon.

## The 1978 City Title — "The Fightingest Team I've Ever Had"

The one that finally broke through arrived in the rain. Angelo had been 0-8-1 in City Title appearances — as a player, as an assistant, and as a head coach. The Public League had won the game only twice (Lincoln 1958, Central 1970) and tied three others since 1951. Frankford, representing that "poor, little, ol' Public League" against Archbishop Wood at Kennedy Stadium, was supposed to take another beating. Instead, on a field that had been hammered into mud-covered slop by a driving rainstorm, the Pioneers handed Wood a 27-7 beating of their own.

Junior fullback Joe Galasso — a transfer from North Catholic — carried 30 times for 155 yards and two touchdowns. Walt Parrish, a two-way lineman who had come to Frankford by way of Olney and Roman Catholic, made four tackles behind the line and recovered the botched pitchout that set up the final score. Quarterback Chris Yurkow ran for one touchdown and threw for another — his only completed pass of the game.

{{PullQuote id="fightingest"}}

Afterward, Angelo refused to make the afternoon about himself. "Above and beyond what this victory means to people at Frankford and to me," he said, "I'm just really happy for the Public League and all of its coaches. To many people, we're just the poor, little, ol' Public League. A couple of weeks ago, someone was quoted as saying, 'Frankford plays the Mastbaums, the Olneys and the Kings while so-and-so plays the Judges, the Doughertys and the somebody elses,' implying, I guess, that we play nobodies. OK, I'll admit we're not as strong overall as the Catholic League, but that remark kind of hurt me. It made me feel bad for Mastbaum, Olney and King, and for their coaches."

Yurkow, who had watched the Public League lose this game on TV and radio his entire life, stood in the rain afterward and said it still hadn't hit him. "Here I am and everybody's celebrating and it still hasn't hit me."

It hit everybody else. Frankford's first City Title since 1940. The celebration at the high school lingered for weeks.

## The Dynasty Continues — 1980, 1981, 1984

Angelo's machine kept running. The 1980 team beat Central 22-18 on a late Scott Croft scramble and finished 10-0, the best record in school history. The 1981 team rolled Washington 34-8 behind George Heineman's four-touchdown afternoon. By 1984, his last season before his first retirement, a senior named Blair Thomas was finishing a career that would send him to Penn State and the NFL first round.

In the 1984 title game, Thomas carried the ball 42 times for 167 yards and a touchdown. Frankford beat Central 16-0. Angelo walked off the field 172-39-5 across 20 seasons with nine Public League titles, and Thomas walked off with city-leagues career records in rushing attempts (626), rushing yards (3,941), rushing touchdowns (55) and total touchdowns (59). When reporters asked Angelo to rank the team, the player, the career — he did what he always did. He refused.

## Retirement, Return, and the 12-0 Season

Angelo stepped down after the 1984 championship. "The coaching, being on the field, is still great," he explained. "It was just some of the other stuff that got to me, wore me out." John McAneney took over and won the 1986 title. But when McAneney retired from teaching the following June, Angelo — who had spent two seasons trying to stay away and failing — eagerly reclaimed his old job at age 57.

{{PhotoInline id="coaching-legends"}}

"I can't say I've missed all football," he told Ted Silary that summer, "but I've definitely missed Frankford football. When I missed it a lot the first year, people would tell me, 'Next year, it won't be as bad.' It was worse. I missed it even more."

What followed was the most famous season in Public League history. Angelo, hobbled by degenerative arthritis in his right hip, coaching through constant pain, led Frankford to a 12-0 record — the first undefeated, untied season any Public League team had ever put together. The Pioneers rewrote the city-leagues single-season points record with 454, topping the 438 Bishop Egan had scored back in 1966. Angelo passed Paul "Bart" Bartolomeo of Southeast Catholic-Bishop Neumann as the winningest coach in city-leagues history. And in the title game, Frankford demolished Dobbins 42-14 behind Darren Swift (19-137, two touchdowns) and Sean Parish (11-106, two touchdowns).

{{PullQuote id="earthquake"}}

In the last six or seven minutes of the title game, Angelo recalled, his starters kept begging to go back in for one final snap, "just so they could come off the field together one last time. They were begging, like little kids." He wouldn't let them. He worried it would look like showmanship, like he was making a circus of the Dobbins coach, his friend Bob Smith. "So we didn't do it."

Asked if 1987 was Frankford's best team, Angelo — as always — refused to rank it. "Frankford isn't a coaches' team," he said. "It's a players' team." He had built that philosophy brick by brick, season by season. Every practice, if a former player wandered by, Angelo stopped the drill, introduced the alum to the current team, and had the kids applaud. He had been doing it for 20 years.

## The Hip Brings Halt to a Legendary Career

A few months after the 12-0 finish, Angelo retired again — this time for good. He had scheduled a hip replacement for February 16, had donated two units of his own blood in preparation, and had then, in his own words, "chickened out." Two months later, his hip was worse than it had been in a decade. "I'm almost stationary," he told Silary. "I can only go a few feet."

He asked to stay on as an assistant to his successor. "I'd still have fun," he said, "but I'd have to do a lot less work. I never liked game day anyway. I was always so nervous, I hardly knew my name. I always liked practice best, just teaching the kids. I really loved practicing on non-school days. It was just football; no distractions."

{{PhotoInline id="reunion"}}

He meant it. He meant all of it. Angelo spent the rest of his life connected to Frankford football — showing up at games, at reunions, at Markward Club luncheons, at every alumnus's wedding and every old player's phone call. In May of 2007, with his pancreatic cancer diagnosis fresh, nearly 300 former players, cheerleaders, coaches, managers and trainers gathered to honor him at the first Frankford Football Alumni Banquet. Angelo called it "one of the nicest days of his life."

## The Legend in His Own Words — and Theirs

The story Joe Schiavo told at Angelo's viewing said more about the man than any stat line could. Schiavo, Angelo's classmate at Frankford and lifelong friend, had helped plan Angelo's funeral — the songs, the pallbearers, all of it — because Angelo wanted to plan it himself, the same way he game-planned Dobbins. Schiavo convinced Angelo to have the service at St. Martin of Tours, a few blocks from Frankford High, and drove Angelo down to meet with Father John Kelly, the pastor.

Father Kelly asked Angelo if he was at peace with dying. Angelo said yes — he'd had a great life. Then Father Kelly asked him about heaven. "I think heaven is going to be a really great place," Angelo said. "I think I'm going to be very happy there."

"Do you think you'll be as happy as you were in 1978," Father Kelly asked, "when you beat Archbishop Wood for the City championship, 27-7?"

Angelo looked over at Schiavo, stunned. Schiavo hadn't said a word about that game. Somehow Father Kelly had just known. Angelo laughed — the kind of laugh a man makes when a memory he'd kept for himself suddenly sits across the room.

The services were held on April 24, 2008. The funeral procession passed Frankford's stadium on the way to St. Dominic Cemetery. It couldn't have gone any other way.
