## The Kid Who Said "I'm Taking Him Deep"

Bob Spinks — Bobby, Bobby Bigz, Spinksie, to the Roxborough-Manayunk kids he grew up with — graduated from Roman Catholic High School in June 2005. He died on December 9, 2005. Between those two dates were six months that included a college season he never got to play, and before those two dates were four years during which he became the kind of high school athlete whose teammates are still quoting him years later.

{{PhotoInline id="spinks-portrait"}}

He was a third baseman, primarily. He could hit a baseball a mile. He was a multi-sport athlete at Roman — football, basketball, baseball — who spent most weekends from grade school forward playing games at Ivy Ridge or the 21st Ward diamonds in Northwest Philly. He wore No. 24 at Roman. He wore No. 27 with the Ivy Ridge Panthers American Legion team in the summers. And the first sentence every friend wrote about him after he died was always some version of the same thing: Bob played 110 percent every single time.

{{PullQuote id="fearless"}}

## The Championship Innings

The story that gets told the most is the championship game where Bobby took the ball on a bad elbow because there was no one else. Adam Seltzer, who pitched alongside him for the Panthers, remembered it in almost exactly those words: "Bobby had a bad elbow injury, so much so that he struggled to even throw on the side. However, we didn't have any other pitchers. He went out there and threw six great innings to lead us to a win. He didn't care how his elbow would feel after the game, he wanted the ball and he wanted the game on his shoulders."

Then, in the championship game itself, Bobby got a game-winning hit to advance the Panthers, and then a run-down play that later scored the insurance run in the title game. The Ivy Ridge Panthers won the Philadelphia County American Legion Championship that summer.

## Senior Year at Roman

Roman baseball coach Joe Tremoglie inherited Bob for his senior year only. One season was enough to leave a mark. Tremoglie remembered a player who "had an intense desire to win and played with a fearlessness that as a coach, you love your players to have" — a kid who "made himself into a starter and vocal leader from the outset." Spinks helped Roman reach the Catholic League baseball semifinals in 2005, his only full season as a varsity starter at the school.

{{PullQuote id="tremoglie-leader"}}

Nick Dorazio, who briefly coached Bob when Bob attended Kennedy-Kenrick for a semester before returning to Roman, had his own memory: when they saw each other again after Bob came back to Roman, Bob "came right over to me and shook my hand like nothing ever happened." He held no grudges. He never carried drama.

## "Big Brother" to Everyone

The rest of the tributes his teammates wrote fell into two categories: fearless-competitor stories and generous-friend stories. Carmen Del Mastro, a classmate at Roman: "He would always try and make people laugh and he just wanted to have a good time no matter what he was doing." Ben Paranzino called him a big brother, a kid who paid for teammates' dinners, who took care of problems when other guys had them.

Jerry Bidus, the No. 10 for Ivy Ridge: "I cannot imagine next year playing without Bob. We'll get it again next year for you, bro."

And Sickel, a football teammate, remembered the quieter side of the same kid who always wanted the ball with the game on the line: "Beginning of senior year I had a close friend die and every day he asked me about it and would ask every day if I was alright. Some people might not have known this side of Bob, but he was a very caring kid even if he didn't know them."

## Remembered

Bob Spinks was 18 years old when he died. His number — Ivy Ridge No. 27, Roman No. 24 — has been retired in the memories of the teammates who played those summers and seasons with him. His parents and his sister and niece remain part of the Ivy Ridge family. And the championship summer of 2005 — the Panthers' Philadelphia County title, the six innings on a bad elbow, the teammate carried down the first-base line on his shoulders — stays the way Adam Seltzer said it would: a moment no one who was there will ever forget.
